Key Takeaways
  • Sleep is a complex biological process that cycles through non-REM and REM sleep several times each night, with each stage serving important functions for the brain and body.
  • Your body’s internal clock, sleep drive, and hormones work together to regulate when you feel sleepy, when you wake up, and how deeply you sleep.
  • While you sleep, the brain sorts memories and the body repairs and restores itself.

Even after decades of research, the exact reason why we sleep remains one of the most enduring and intriguing mysteries in health science. To try to get to the bottom of this question, experts analyze how sleep works and what happens when we don’t get enough sleep.

Sleep affects almost every body system and depends on hormones and chemicals made by several brain regions, and research has mapped much of what goes on overnight.

What Happens When You Sleep?

Soon after you nod off, body temperature, brain activity, heart rate and breathing drop, and energy use falls.

Each night brings several 70- to 120-minute cycles made up of distinct sleep stages.

How Does the Body Regulate Sleep?

Two drivers control sleep: the circadian alerting system and sleep-wake homeostasis.

  • Sleep-wake homeostasis: the urge to sleep grows the longer you stay awake.
  • The circadian alerting system: roughly 24-hour circadian rhythms, set mainly by light.

Stress, hunger, caffeine and screen light can disrupt both.

Many brain areas, from the hypothalamus to the cerebral cortex, take part.

What Chemicals and Hormones Regulate Sleep?

Many chemicals and hormones drive the switch between sleep and waking.

Adenosine builds up while you are awake and seems to raise sleep pressure; caffeine suppresses it.

Melatonin, released as light fades, is the best-known sleep hormone; sleep in turn affects growth and appetite hormones.

Genetic differences can alter this signaling, so disorders like sleep apnea may run in families; environment and lifestyle matter too.

Why Is Sleep Important?

Experts have not settled why we sleep, but evidence points to a vital biological role.

Sleep aids development in young people, and in adults too little of it is linked to heart problems, weaker immunity, obesity, type 2 diabetes, impaired thinking and memory, and depression or anxiety.
